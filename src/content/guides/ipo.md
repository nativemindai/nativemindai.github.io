## The short version
Before a US company first sells shares to the public (an IPO), it files a document with the SEC, the US markets regulator, that explains its business, its money and its risks.
You can read it for free, and an AI chatbot can put it in plain English for you.

## Do this
1. Copy this prompt:

   ```prompt
   I'm reading part of a company's IPO filing (Form S-1). Explain the text I paste below in plain English, for someone new to investing:

   1. What the company does, in two sentences.
   2. How it makes money.
   3. What it plans to do with the money from the IPO.
   4. The three biggest risks, one line each.
   5. Anything unusual that's worth a closer look.

   Keep it short. Don't tell me whether to buy or sell. Just explain.

   Here's the text:
   ```

2. Paste it into any AI chatbot app, like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. Don't send it yet.
3. Tap **SEC EDGAR search** below, type the company's name in the **Company name, ticker…** box and tap **SEARCH**.
4. Tap the most recent filing marked **S-1** or **S-1/A** (an updated version). Check the **Filed** date.
5. Copy the first few paragraphs of the **Prospectus Summary**, near the start of the filing: press and hold, drag to select, then tap **Copy**.
6. Paste them into the chatbot under your prompt and send.

## More prompts
To check the deal itself, copy the cover page (the very first page of the filing) and use this prompt the same way:

```prompt
Below is the cover page of [COMPANY NAME]'s IPO filing. In plain English, tell me:

1. How many shares are being sold, and at what price or price range.
2. Which stock exchange the shares will trade on, and the ticker.
3. Whether any existing shareholders are selling their own shares.
4. Which banks are running the IPO.

If something isn't in the text, say so instead of guessing.

Here's the text:
```

## Watch out
- **The SEC doesn't vouch for the company.** Its review doesn't judge whether an IPO is a good investment, and it doesn't guarantee the filing is complete or accurate.
- **Read the newest version.** Companies update the filing along the way (S-1/A), so an older version can be out of date.
- **Most IPO shares usually go to big clients first**, like funds and wealthy investors. Most people can only buy once the shares start trading.
- **The price can swing.** Soon after an IPO, the price can end up well above or below the IPO price. When early shareholders' lock-up ends (often 180 days later), the price may drop if many of them sell.
- **AI can get things wrong.** Check any number that matters against the filing itself, or read the SEC's own guide to IPOs below.
